REAL-WORLD COMPARISONS

Look the same. Weigh differently.

Put two Cuban link chains side by side — same width, same length, same karat on the tag — and they can still come in grams apart on a scale. That gap is not trivia: melt value is computed from weight, so a weight difference is a money difference. This page walks through documented comparisons from weights manufacturers publish themselves.

Where these numbers come from

On October 31, 2026, we collected 79 weights that makers and sellers publish for their own Cuban link chains — spec tables, catalog listings, and product pages — from 8 sources including GOLDZENN, TraxNYC, Popular Jewelry, and Direct Source Gold & Diamond. Seventy-seven were consistent enough to use; two were excluded, for reasons explained below. Every published weight here is named with its maker, and our model's figure is always labeled an estimate — the two are never blended, the same discipline used on the weight chart.

The overall picture first: across the 77 usable figures, the median published weight sits +1.6% from our model's prediction, and 83% fall within ±10% of it — a sound center. The comparisons below are about the spread around that center, because in two real quotes, the spread is what you are paying for or missing.

Comparison 1: the same 14K chain, two makers, 14 grams apart

Take the most standard specification in the dataset: a solid 14K chain, 10mm wide, 24 inches long. GOLDZENN's published spec table lists it at 185 g; TraxNYC's published median for its own solid 14K listings at the same size is 199 g. On paper the two chains are identical. On a scale they differ by 14 grams — about 7.6% — and 14 grams of gold is 14 grams of melt value present in one quote and not the other.

Neither maker is "wrong." At 5mm × 22 in, a third source — Direct Source Gold & Diamond's listing at 47.2 g — sits above both, and the direction is not fixed: at 10mm TraxNYC runs heavier than GOLDZENN, at 5mm lighter. Each shop's link profile, tightness, filing, and clasp add up differently. GOLDZENN states a ±5–7% handmade weight tolerance on its product pages — one maker's own chains are expected to vary by about the size of the gap between makers.

Comparison 2: at 10K and 8mm, two makers agree — the estimate reads light

Sometimes the interesting comparison is not maker against maker, but both makers against the model. For a solid 10K chain at 8mm × 24 in, our estimate is ≈ 102 g. GOLDZENN's spec table says 116 g (+13.3%). TraxNYC's published grams-per-inch figures work out to 112.1 g (+9.5%). GOLDZENN's separate 22-inch catalog table tells the same story: 108 g published against a ≈ 94 g estimate (+15.1%).

Two independent makers, publishing in different formats, land in the same place: a real 10K chain at this width carries roughly 10–15% more weight than a density-scaled estimate predicts. Across all twelve published 10K figures, the median sits about 7% above the model. A seller's 10K chain outweighing a dimension-based estimate is not, by itself, a discrepancy — it is the documented pattern for this karat.

Comparison 3: at statement widths, real chains come in lighter

The spread flips direction at the wide end. Direct Source Gold & Diamond lists a solid 14K chain at 15mm × 26 in at 380 g. Our model, which scales weight with the square of the width, predicts ≈ 450 g — the published chain is 70 g lighter (−15.6%). A larger example points the same way: CRM Jewelers documents a custom build at 33mm × 28.5 in, 14K, final weight 2,086 g against a prediction of ≈ 2,390 g (−12.7%).

The likely mechanics: at very wide sizes, a link's thickness and height do not keep growing in proportion to its width, so a width-squared law overshoots. Hold the conclusion loosely — only four figures in the dataset sit above 13mm, so this is a documented signal, not a settled curve. It is also why the weight chart labels its widest cells as estimates with thin published backing.

Comparison 4: at 3mm, a fifth of the weight is not in the links

At the thin end the absolute numbers are small and the percentage is large. TraxNYC's published median for a solid 14K chain at 3mm × 24 in is 20 g; the model predicts ≈ 16.6 g — the published chain is 3.4 g heavier, a +20.3% difference. A clasp weighs roughly what a clasp weighs whether the chain is 3mm or 10mm, so on a fine chain it is a far larger share of the total, and stated widths at this size are rounded more coarsely, pushing the same direction. For chains at 4mm and under, expect a scale to read above a dimension-based estimate.

What actually moves the weight

Width, squared — and width tolerance. Weight grows with the square of the width, because a wider link is also a thicker, taller one. Small measurement differences therefore matter: a chain measuring 10.5mm instead of 10mm carries roughly 10% more cross-section. Makers also hold tolerances — GOLDZENN's stated ±5–7% handmade tolerance is the one documented figure in the dataset — so two chains sold as "10mm" may not measure, or weigh, the same.

Link profile, tightness, and filing. Cuban links are flattened and filed after forming, and how much metal is filed away — and how tight and flat each maker's profile sits — differs by shop. This is reasoning rather than a measured figure, but it is the most plausible account of Comparison 1: two honest makers, one specification, a 14-gram gap that flips direction at other widths.

The clasp's share. Documented in Comparison 4: hardware is a fixed weight riding on a variable chain. The finer the chain, the larger the clasp's percentage — +20.3% at 3mm, a rounding error at 10mm.

Hollow cores — documented gap, then reasoning. Documented: GOLDZENN sells hollow Cuban links in the 3–7.5mm range but publishes no per-size gram weights for them, and across all 8 sources here there is not one published hollow weight — which is why the weight chart covers solid chains only. One listing was excluded for exactly this ambiguity: a 7.5mm 10K chain listed at 22.1 g, far below every solid figure near that width, construction not stated. Reasoning: a hollow link is a tube — same outside dimensions, metal only in the walls — so it necessarily weighs less than a solid chain of the same size, by an amount set by a wall thickness no maker here publishes. We put no number on it, because there is no sourced number to put.

Karat density. Higher-karat alloys are denser, so the same chain weighs more before purity even enters the melt calculation. GOLDZENN's spec table makes the ladder concrete at 10mm × 24 in: 157 g in 10K, 185 g in 14K, 225 g in 18K — one maker, one size, a 68-gram spread from karat alone. The densities behind this are on the methodology page (10K at 11.6 g/cm³, 18K at 15.5 g/cm³).

How to use this when you compare quotes

When two quotes describe what looks like the same chain, work through the weight before the price. Ask each seller for the actual weight in grams — from a scale, not a size estimate — and for the construction, stated plainly: solid or hollow. Then put both quotes into the quote comparison tool: it works out each quote's price per gram, melt value at the same spot price, and the Premium over Melt as a separate layer, so a heavier chain and a higher markup stop looking like the same thing. Finally, check the size against the weight chart — a quote whose weight sits far outside the published spread for its size is a quote worth asking about.

Questions this page answers a lot

Does a heavier chain mean a better chain?

Not by itself. At the same karat, more grams means more gold content, and melt value follows the grams — that part is arithmetic. How well a chain is made is a separate question weight cannot answer, and this site does not grade one maker's work against another's.

Why did two figures from the same maker get excluded from the dataset?

Because they contradicted that maker's own tables. GOLDZENN's investment-guide prose describes a 14K 8mm chain at 22 inches as "approximately 190 g" and a 12mm at "approximately 366 g," while its catalog table on the same page gives 118 g and 245 g. When a source disagrees with itself, we keep the structured tables, set the prose aside, and say so.

Can you tell me what a hollow chain of a given size weighs?

No — and be cautious with any source that answers confidently. No manufacturer in our dataset publishes per-size hollow weights, so there is no documented figure to chart. Weigh the actual chain and value it on its measured weight.
