Start with the metal, not the ticket price

A chain is worth its gold first and its markup second. A ticket price mixes metal, labor, design, overhead and profit into one number; a selling offer mixes metal with deductions and margin. This calculator pulls the layers apart so each can be judged on its own terms.

Use it for a chain, bracelet, ring, coin or piece of scrap. Enter a measured weight, choose the karat or type a hallmark, and the tool returns the gold-content value at the current spot price. Then model what a store premium does to a buying price, or what a buyer's percentage and fees do to a payout. The metal value stays the same either way; only the layer added to it, or taken from it, changes.

Three figures, three jobs

Melt value is the starting line. It is the value of the pure gold inside an item: net weight multiplied by purity, multiplied by the market price of one gram of pure gold. It is not a promise that anyone will pay that amount for a finished piece, and it is not a store's selling price. It is the shared reference point that makes different prices comparable.

A store premium is everything charged above that metal value for a finished item. It can include making the piece, finishing and presenting it, running the store and earning a profit. The calculator does not split a premium into those parts, because a single price cannot prove how much belongs to each one. It simply shows the size of the layer, in dollars and as a percentage.

A buyer payout runs in the other direction. A buyer may offer a percentage of melt and subtract separate fees. Modeling the percentage and the fees apart shows what is actually being offered: an offer can look close to melt before a fee and land noticeably lower after it. Keeping the three figures separate is the whole point; confusing them means arguing about different numbers while using the same words.

How to use the calculator

  1. Pick the karat, or type the hallmark from the piece. A fineness entry replaces the karat buttons until you choose a karat again.
  2. Enter the weight and its unit. Grams, pennyweight, troy ounces and tola are supported, and switching units preserves the same physical weight.
  3. Subtract non-gold weight only when you know it — a stone's weight you measured, for example. Leave the box empty rather than guessing.
  4. Check the price state — the bar and the market tile label it live, manual, example or stale. Comparing a quote from another day? Type that day's price into the spot box.
  5. Read the melt value first. Then open the buying layer to test a premium or check a real quote, or switch to the selling layer to model a percentage and fees.

Karat is a fraction. Fineness is a count.

Karat describes gold as a fraction of twenty-four parts. Fourteen-karat gold is fourteen parts out of twenty-four, or about 58.3% gold; the rest is other metals added by the maker. Fineness says the same thing as parts per thousand: a 750 stamp means 75%, the fineness form of 18K.

The two systems do not always line up to the last digit. Nominal 14K is 583 parts per thousand, while a common stamp reads 585. A real difference, just a small one: at the same weight and spot price, 585 values slightly higher than 583 because it states slightly more gold.

That is why the fineness box exists. When a piece carries a tested or trusted stamp — 375, 585, 750, 916 or 999 — enter it and let the exact fraction drive the result. The calculator models 24K as 99.9% pure, matching the 999 fineness used for fine gold, rather than pretending any real item is literally 100% gold.

A worked example, with round numbers

Example only — round teaching numbers, not a market quote. Set the spot price to $4,000 per troy ounce and weigh a 100 g piece stamped 585. One gram of pure gold is worth $4,000 divided by 31.1034768, about $128.60. At 58.5% purity, each gram of the piece carries about $75.23 of gold, so the melt value is about $7,523.

Now give the same piece two futures. A store quotes $9,400: that is about $1,877 above melt, a premium of about 24.9% — roughly $94.00 per gram against $75.23 of gold per gram. A buyer offers 85% of melt with a $25 fee: 85% is about $6,395, and the fee brings the payout to about $6,370. Same piece, same melt — three different numbers, each answering a different question.

What the result leaves out

The calculator values gold content only. It does not price gemstones, brand, design, condition or the work a maker put into a piece, and it does not add tax or shipping to a store scenario. A finished chain can reasonably sell above melt, and an intact piece can be worth more to a reseller than as scrap. Use melt as the floor reference every other figure is measured against — not as a claim about what any item must cost or fetch.

Gold calculator questions

Why did the value move when I entered a stamp instead of tapping a karat?

Because the stamp can state a slightly different fraction. A 585 stamp is 58.5% gold; nominal 14K is about 58.3%. Same weight, same spot price — a touch more gold in the math.

Should I weigh a piece with its stones still in it?

Weigh what you can identify. If part of the total is stone, glass or steel and you know that weight, subtract it in the deduction box. If you do not know it, the result treats the full weight as gold alloy and will read high.

Why is the per-gram value lower for 10K than for 18K?

Each gram of 10K contains less gold — about 41.7%, against 75% for 18K. The spot price is the same; the fraction of each gram that is actually gold is not.

Can a chain be worth more than its melt value?

As a finished piece, yes. Craftsmanship, brand, condition and demand can carry a resale price above the metal. Melt is the value of the gold alone — a reference point, not a ceiling.

Why might a buyer's final payment differ from the first offer?

An offer may assume a weight or fineness that testing revises, or it may be quoted before fees. Ask whether the figure is before or after deductions, and confirm the tested weight and stamp.
