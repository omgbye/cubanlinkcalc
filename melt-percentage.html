Why offers land below melt

Melt value is what the gold is worth as metal. A buyer who pays you for a chain still has to turn that chain back into money, and the steps in between cost them something. The gap between melt and your payout is where those costs — and the buyer's margin — live:

  • Assay and testing. Before paying, a buyer verifies that the metal is what the stamp claims — XRF scanning, acid testing, sometimes a melt assay. Equipment, time, and the occasional bad piece all cost money.
  • Refining. A chain is an alloy, not pure gold. Refining separates the gold back out, and refiners charge for it; the buyer either pays that charge or sells the alloy at a discount that reflects it.
  • Price risk. The spot price moves every day. A buyer pays you at today's price but may not sell the metal until days or weeks later, at whatever price the market gives them then. Part of the gap is the cost of carrying that risk.
  • Running a business. Rent, staff, insurance, security, and the simple fact that a business has to earn more than it spends. The margin is not separate from the gap — it is part of it.

None of that makes any particular percentage the right one. It means the honest question is never "why isn't this 100%?" but "what am I getting for the percentage I'm giving up — speed, certainty, a tested and documented payout?" That is why the percentage, and especially the net percentage after fees, is the number worth comparing.

Comparing two buyers

Two offers quoted in different formats — one as a percentage of melt, one as a flat dollar figure with fees — cannot be compared by headline. Run each one through this checker on the same day, against the same spot price, and compare the net % of melt: the percentage you actually keep after that buyer's fees come out. A higher headline percentage with heavy fees can pay less than a lower one with none.

Ask each buyer which of these applies before you compare:

  • Assay / testing fee — charged per piece or per batch, and whether you pay it if you decline the offer.
  • Refining charge — a flat fee or a percentage taken off the top for refining the alloy.
  • Shipping and insurance — for mail-in offers, who pays to ship, how the package is insured, and who pays for the return if you say no.
  • Minimum payout thresholds — some buyers pay a lower percentage below a minimum weight or value, or hold small payouts until a threshold is met.

Get the answers in writing where you can, enter each buyer's real fee total, and let the net percentages sit side by side. That is the whole comparison — the same chain, the same gold price, and the only variable left is what each buyer actually pays.
