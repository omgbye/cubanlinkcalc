GOLD KNOWLEDGE

What is melt value?

Melt value is the worth of the gold itself — the number underneath every buying price and every selling offer. Once you can compute it, no jeweler, pawn shop, or scrap buyer can quote you a number you cannot check.

The definition

Melt value is the value of an item's gold content at the current market price of gold, called the spot price. The name comes from what the number assumes: if the piece were melted down and refined back into pure gold, this is what that gold would be worth. It deliberately ignores craftsmanship, brand, gemstones, and condition. A handmade chain and a broken chain with the same weight and purity have the same melt value, because melt only measures metal.

The formula has three parts:

  • Weight in grams (convert if needed: one troy ounce is 31.1035 grams, one pennyweight is 1.555 grams, one tola is 11.6638 grams).
  • Purity as a fraction — 14K is 58.3%, 18K is 75%, and a 585 stamp means 58.5%.
  • Spot price per gram of pure gold, which is the spot price per troy ounce divided by 31.1035.

Multiply the three together and you have melt value. That is exactly the calculation the calculator runs with the live spot price, and it is the baseline for everything else on this site: a store's price is melt plus a premium, and a buyer's offer is melt minus a deduction.

A worked example

The numbers below are an illustration with round figures, not today's price — use the calculator for the live number. Suppose the spot price were exactly $4,000 per troy ounce. Pure gold would then be worth about $128.60 per gram. A 14K gram, at 58.3% purity, would be worth about $75.02. Now take a solid 14K Cuban link weighing 100 grams: its melt value would be roughly $7,500.

Hold that $7,500 figure and watch what different people do with it. A retailer selling that chain new might price it well above melt, because the price includes making the chain. A scrap buyer might offer 90% of melt — about $6,750 — and then subtract a small testing or processing fee, leaving a check around $6,725. A pawn shop might offer far less, because a pawn offer is built around a loan or a quick resale, not around refining metal. Same chain, same melt value, three very different numbers. Melt is the constant; the other figures are business decisions layered on top of it.

How scrap buyers and pawn shops price

A scrap buyer's process is mechanical, and knowing the steps lets you follow along instead of watching a number appear from nowhere. They weigh the piece. They test the fineness — with an XRF scanner, an acid test, or both — because a stamp is a claim, not proof. They compute melt at the day's spot price. Then they offer a percentage of melt, keeping the remainder as their margin and refining cost, and sometimes subtracting explicit fees on top.

That is why "we pay top dollar for gold" means nothing and "we pay X% of melt" means everything. Percentages vary by buyer, by how much gold you are selling, and by how clean the metal is. Larger, well-documented sales generally command better percentages than a single small item. Pieces with stones, steel springs in clasps, or other non-gold components get deductions for the non-gold weight, which is fair — as long as the deduction is stated and reasonable.

Pawn shops sit at the low end of the range for a structural reason: many pawn transactions are loans, not purchases, and even their purchase offers price in the risk and cost of reselling the item from a shop shelf. A desirable, intact Cuban link in a popular size can be worth more than scrap to a reseller or a private buyer, because they are buying a chain, not raw material. Melt is the floor of that conversation, not the ceiling.

Why melt is not the check you get

Sellers are often disappointed the first time an offer lands below melt, but the gap is not automatically a scam. Refining gold costs money. Testing costs money. The buyer carries the risk that the spot price falls between buying your chain and selling the refined metal. And every buyer in the chain expects a margin for doing business. What you can control is the size of the gap: it shrinks when you know your melt value before you walk in, when your weight and fineness are documented, and when more than one buyer is competing for the same piece.

The selling view on the calculator models exactly this: enter a buyer's percentage of melt and any additional fee, and it shows the resulting payout. If you already have an offer, it also tells you what percentage of melt that offer represents — the fastest way to compare two offers quoted in different formats.

Selling tips that protect the number

  • Weigh it yourself first. A jewelry scale is inexpensive, and knowing the weight before anyone quotes you removes the easiest misunderstanding.
  • Know your melt value before you negotiate. Run the weight and karat through the calculator at the live spot price on the day you sell, not last week's price.
  • Get at least three offers and ask each buyer what percentage of melt they are paying and what fees apply. Compare the final amounts, not the slogans.
  • Keep gemstones separate in the conversation. Melt covers gold only. Stones should be removed, returned, or valued on their own — never quietly counted as gold weight or silently kept.
  • Consider selling an intact chain as a chain. A clean, solid Cuban link in a sought-after size can beat its scrap value in a private sale or to a reseller, because the buyer is paying for the piece, not just the metal.
  • Be careful with mail-in buyers. If you ship gold, document weight, fineness, and condition with photos first, and confirm the return policy in writing before the package leaves your hands.

The habit to build: never discuss a price for gold — buying or selling — until melt value is on the table. It takes thirty seconds on the calculator, and it turns every negotiation into arithmetic you can follow. For the buying side of the same coin, read how to buy a Cuban link chain.
