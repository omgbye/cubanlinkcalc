GOLD KNOWLEDGE

Gold karats, explained.

The karat number is the single most important number on a piece of gold jewelry: it tells you exactly how much of the metal is actually gold. Here is how karats, fineness, and hallmark stamps work, and how to read what is stamped on your chain.

What a karat actually measures

Pure gold is measured as 24 parts. A karat is one of those parts, so the karat number tells you how many twenty-fourths of the metal are gold. 14K gold is 14 parts gold and 10 parts other metals — 58.3% gold by weight. 18K is 18 parts gold, or 75%. 24K is effectively pure gold, modeled on this site as 99.9%.

Why mix metals in at all? Pure gold is soft — soft enough to bend and scratch in daily use. Alloying it with stronger metals makes jewelry that can survive being worn. The trade is always the same: lower karat means less gold, a lower value per gram, and a harder, more durable piece. Higher karat means more gold, a richer color, a higher value per gram, and a softer piece that needs a little more care.

At the same weight and the same spot price, value scales directly with purity: a gram of 18K contains exactly 75% of a gram of pure gold, so it is worth 75% of the pure per-gram price. That is the math behind the live value-per-gram table on the calculator page.

Fineness and hallmarks: the same fact, written differently

Fineness states purity in parts per thousand instead of parts per twenty-four. The two systems describe the same thing: 750 fineness is 75% gold, which is 18K. Hallmark stamps on jewelry are almost always fineness numbers — 375, 585, 750, 916, 999 — sometimes alongside a karat mark and a maker's mark. When you find a three-digit stamp on a clasp or link, you can enter it directly into the fineness box on the calculator and it will value the piece at that exact purity rather than a nominal karat.

Two cautions about stamps. First, a stamp is a claim made by whoever marked the piece; by itself it does not prove authenticity. Buyers who need certainty test the metal, typically with XRF analysis or an acid test. Second, some stamps are warnings in disguise: marks such as "GP" (gold plated), "HGE" (heavy gold electroplate), or "GF" (gold filled) mean the piece has a surface layer or bonded layer of gold over base metal, not solid gold, and its melt value is a small fraction of a solid piece of the same size.

The 583 vs 585 detail

Nominal 14K is 14 divided by 24, which is 58.33% — a fineness of 583. So why do so many 14K pieces carry a 585 stamp? Because a piece stamped 585 is guaranteeing at least 58.5% gold, which is slightly more than nominal 14K requires. Makers stamp 585 to clear the legal threshold for 14K with a small safety margin, so the piece can never test under. The difference is small — two-tenths of a percent — but on a heavy chain it is real money, and it is why this site lets you value a piece by its exact stamp instead of rounding it to a karat. When precision matters, use the tested fineness, not the karat name.

Alloys, color, and durability

The non-gold portion of the alloy is not filler chosen at random; it sets the color and much of the hardness. Yellow gold alloys typically blend copper and silver, which keep the warm yellow tone. More copper pushes the color toward rose gold. White gold is alloyed with palladium or nickel to mute the yellow — nickel-based white gold is worth knowing about if your skin reacts to nickel. Higher copper content also tends to make an alloy harder, which is part of why lower karats shrug off daily wear better.

Durability is not only about karat, though. Construction matters as much: a solid 14K link will outlast a hollow 10K link in most real-world abuse, because there is simply more metal resisting the dent. Karat tells you what the metal is; construction tells you how much of it is there. For a detailed comparison of constructions, see the buyer's guide.

Which karat for a Cuban link?

For most buyers the answer is the one the market already settled on: 14K. It carries enough gold to hold serious value, keeps a rich color, resists daily wear, and has the deepest resale market of any karat in the United States. Choose 10K if budget or hard daily use comes first and you accept less gold content per gram. Choose 18K if color and gold content matter more than scratch resistance and you are comfortable paying for both the higher purity and the extra weight that usually comes with it. Whatever you choose, verify the weight and the stamp, and price the piece from its melt value up — that is the habit this site is built around.

Try it: find the stamp on your chain's clasp, enter it in the fineness box on the calculator, and compare the exact-stamp value against the nominal karat value. On a heavy chain, the difference is worth seeing.
