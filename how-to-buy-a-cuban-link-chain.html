CUBAN LINK BUYING GUIDE

How to buy a Cuban link chain without overpaying.

A Cuban link is one of the few jewelry purchases you can price almost like a commodity — if you know the two numbers every quote is built from. This guide walks through how the pricing works, what separates a fair premium from a padded one, and the checks to run before money changes hands.

Every price is two numbers

Strip away the display case and every Cuban link chain is priced in two layers. The first layer is melt value: the value of the gold content itself at the current market (spot) price. It is a simple calculation — the chain's weight in grams, multiplied by its purity, multiplied by the price of pure gold per gram. One troy ounce is 31.1035 grams, and gold is quoted per troy ounce, so the per-gram price of any karat follows directly from the live spot price.

The second layer is the premium over melt: everything the seller adds on top of the gold — the labor of making the chain, the clasp, finishing, rent, staff, brand, and profit. On this site we keep those layers separate on purpose. Melt is the metal. The premium is the store's layer, stated as its own number, so you can see exactly what you are paying for the gold and what you are paying for everything else.

Neither number alone tells you whether a price is good. A chain can carry a high premium and be worth it — hand-filed Miami links are genuinely labor-intensive — or carry the same premium for a mass-produced chain that should cost far less to make. What melt value gives you is a floor and a measuring stick: once you know the gold content value, every quote becomes comparable, because you can express each one as a price per gram and a percentage over melt.

Weight is where the money is

Gold is sold by weight, so the single most important fact about any chain is how many grams it contains. Width and length are what you see; grams are what you pay for. Two chains that look identical in a photo can differ by 30% in weight if one is solid and the other is hollow, and that difference is worth hundreds or thousands of dollars.

This is why serious sellers state the weight and why you should always ask for it. With a weight, a karat, and the live spot price, you can compute melt value in seconds on the calculator. If you are shopping by dimensions instead — say, a 10mm chain at 24 inches — the build estimator gives you an estimated weight for a solid Miami Cuban link, calibrated against published maker specifications. As a reference point, a solid 14K chain at 12mm wide and 24 inches long runs roughly 265 grams. Treat every dimension-based estimate as a range, not an exact figure: link profile, spacing, and clasp all shift the real weight, which is why a measured weight from a scale always beats an estimate.

What drives the premium

Premiums vary widely, and understanding what legitimately drives them helps you judge a quote instead of just reacting to it. The main factors:

  • How the chain is made. True Miami Cuban links are drawn, filed, and assembled by hand, and that labor is the biggest honest cost above the metal. Machine-made and cast chains cost less to produce and should carry a smaller premium.
  • The clasp. A well-made box clasp with a secure tongue-and-safety mechanism takes real work. It is part of the chain's weight and part of its price.
  • Finishing. Tight, evenly filed links with a clean polish take time. Sloppy gaps and rough links are signs of cheaper production.
  • Brand and overhead. A famous name, a flagship store, and a warranty program all get priced in. That is not automatically a rip-off — but you should know you are paying for it, and how much.
  • Karat and construction. Higher karats cost more per gram because they contain more gold, and solid construction costs more than hollow at the same size because there is simply more metal.

What a premium number cannot tell you is the seller's actual profit. The premium measures the total gap between metal value and price; it does not break out rent, labor, or margin. Use it to compare sellers against each other, not to accuse any one seller of a specific profit.

Choosing a karat for daily wear

Most Cuban links are sold in 10K, 14K, or 18K, and the choice is a real trade-off. 10K (41.7% gold) is the hardest and most affordable per gram, because most of the metal is stronger alloy. It is a practical choice for a large chain worn hard every day. 14K (58.3% gold) is the Miami standard for a reason: noticeably richer color than 10K, still durable enough for daily wear, and the deepest resale market. 18K (75% gold) has the richest color and the most gold value per gram, but it is softer, costs more, and is less common in big Cuban links.

One detail surprises many buyers: at the same dimensions, a higher-karat chain is usually heavier, because gold alloys get denser as purity rises. So an 18K chain is not just priced higher per gram — there are typically more grams. Compare chains by total weight and total price, never by dimensions alone. Solid, semi-hollow, and hollow

Construction changes both weight and ownership experience. A solid chain is the benchmark this site's estimator models: maximum weight, maximum durability, and the easiest to repair and resell. Semi-hollow and hollow chains look the same size for much less weight, which makes them cheaper — and there is nothing wrong with buying one knowingly at a hollow price. The problems start when a hollow chain is priced like a solid one. Hollow links also dent more easily and can be harder to repair. Always ask which construction you are being quoted, and weigh the actual piece whenever you can.

How to check any quote in two minutes

  1. Get the chain's exact weight in grams and its karat or hallmark from the seller.
  2. Enter the weight and karat in the calculator. Note the melt value and the value per gram for that karat.
  3. Type the seller's pre-tax quote into the quote checker. It shows you the premium in dollars and as a percentage, plus the price per gram you would actually be paying.
  4. Repeat with a second and third seller for the same specification — same karat, same dimensions, same construction. Compare pre-tax prices on the same basis.
  5. Decide with the full picture: the lowest premium is not automatically the best chain, but now the difference between quotes is a number you can weigh against workmanship, warranty, and trust.

Questions to ask the jeweler

  • What is the exact weight in grams, and can I see it on the scale?
  • Is it solid, semi-hollow, or hollow?
  • What fineness is it stamped — 583, 585, or something else?
  • Is it handmade or machine-made, and where was it made?
  • What kind of clasp does it have?
  • What is the price per gram?
  • What are the return policy and the buy-back policy if I sell it back later?
  • Is the quoted price before or after tax?

A good jeweler answers these without hesitation. The answers are basic product facts, and any seller of a multi-thousand-dollar gold chain should know every one of them.

Red flags

  • No weight available. A seller who will not state or show the weight is asking you to buy blind.
  • Price quoted only "per chain" with resistance to breaking it down by gram.
  • A price below melt value for a supposedly solid chain. Gold cannot be sold below its metal value for long; a below-melt price usually means the piece is hollow, plated, lower karat than claimed, or not gold at all.
  • Vague stamps. Markings like "14K GP" or "HGE" mean gold plated, not solid gold. A plain karat stamp is a claim, not a guarantee — testing is what verifies it.
  • Today-only pressure. The spot price moves daily, but a legitimate quote can be re-issued tomorrow. Pressure to decide on the spot is a sales tactic, not a market fact.
  • No returns, no paperwork. You should leave with an itemized receipt stating karat, weight, and price.

The bottom line: weigh it, know its melt value, and make the seller's premium a visible number. Then the negotiation is about workmanship and trust — not about guessing what the gold is worth. Start with the calculator, and read what melt value really means before you sell anything.
